Diagonalizable Groups

A diagonalisable group is an affine algebraic group whose coordinate ring is generated by its characters as a \(k\)-module. Equivalently: it becomes (after base change) a closed subgroup of a torus. Diagonalisable groups generalise tori to include non-connected multiplicative-type groups like \(\mu_n\) and twisted forms thereof.

Definition

Let \(G\) be an affine algebraic group over a field \(k\) (or over a base scheme). \(G\) is diagonalisable if the natural map \[ k[X^*(G)] \;\longrightarrow\; \mathcal{O}(G) \] is an isomorphism, where \(X^*(G) = \operatorname{Hom}_{k\text{-grp}}(G, \mathbb{G}_m)\) is the character group and \(k[X^*(G)]\) is its group algebra.

Equivalently: \(G\) becomes isomorphic to a closed subgroup of a torus \(\mathbb{G}_m^n\) after base change to \(\bar k\), and the embedding descends to \(k\).

Equivalent description via group of multiplicative type

A group of multiplicative type over \(k\) is the Cartier dual of a finitely generated abelian group (regarded as a constant group scheme). Specifically: for a finitely generated abelian group \(M\), the diagonalisable group \(D(M)\) has: \[ \mathcal{O}(D(M)) \;=\; k[M], \qquad D(M)(R) = \operatorname{Hom}(M, R^\times). \]

This gives an anti-equivalence of categories: \[ \{\text{diagonalisable } k\text{-groups}\} \;\longleftrightarrow\; \{\text{finitely generated abelian groups}\}. \]

Examples

\(M\) \(D(M)\)
\(\mathbb{Z}\) \(\mathbb{G}_m\)
\(\mathbb{Z}^n\) \(\mathbb{G}_m^n\)
\(\mathbb{Z}/n\) \(\mu_n\)
\(\mathbb{Z}^n \times (\mathbb{Z}/m)^k\) \(\mathbb{G}_m^n \times \mu_m^k\)

For \(k\) of characteristic \(p\) and \(n = p\): \(D(\mathbb{Z}/p) = \mu_p\) is non-smooth (\(\mu_p\) has nilpotent functions on it). Outside characteristic \(p\), all diagonalisable groups are smooth.

Properties

Let \(G = D(M)\) be a diagonalisable group over a field \(k\):

  • \(G\) is affine, of multiplicative type;
  • \(G\) is smooth iff \(\mathrm{char}(k) \nmid |M_{\mathrm{tors}}|\);
  • \(G^\circ\) (identity component) is the maximal torus \(D(M/M_{\mathrm{tors}})\);
  • \(\pi_0(G) = D(M_{\mathrm{tors}})\), a finite group of multiplicative type.

Twisted forms and groups of multiplicative type

A group of multiplicative type is a diagonalisable group's \(\bar k\)-form: an affine algebraic group \(G\) over \(k\) such that \(G_{\bar k}\) is diagonalisable.

Forms are classified by Galois cohomology of the automorphism group of \(M\): \[ \{\text{forms of } D(M) \text{ over } k\} \;\cong\; H^1(k, \operatorname{Aut}(M)). \]

For \(M = \mathbb{Z}^n\): forms of \(\mathbb{G}_m^n\) are exactly tori of dimension \(n\) over \(k\) (classified by \(\operatorname{GL}_n(\mathbb{Z})\)-cocycles).

Splitting

A diagonalisable group is split (over \(k\)) if \(M\) is a trivial Galois module, i.e.\ \(G\) is literally a closed subgroup of \(\mathbb{G}_m^n\) over \(k\) (not just over \(\bar k\)).

For every diagonalisable group \(G\), there exists a finite separable extension \(K/k\) such that \(G_K\) is split — the splitting field.

Functoriality

The functor \(M \mapsto D(M)\): - exact: short exact sequences in \(M\) correspond to short exact sequences in \(D(M)\); - compatible with base change: \(D(M)_S = D(M)_{\text{Spec } S}\).

This makes diagonalisable groups especially tractable: classification, classification of subgroups, classification of quotients, etc.\ all reduce to abelian-group questions.

Role in reductive structure

  • Maximal tori are diagonalisable groups; their character lattices and Galois actions give the root datum of a reductive group.
  • Centres of reductive groups are groups of multiplicative type (in general non-connected, e.g.\ \(\mu_n \subseteq \mathrm{SL}_n\)).
  • Diagonalizable subgroups of \(G\) are completely classified for \(G\) reductive (Borel-Steinberg).
